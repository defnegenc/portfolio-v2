How kilims are woven

A kilim is a flatweave: it has no pile. Wool warp threads are stretched on a loom, and coloured weft threads are passed over and under them, then beaten down until they hide the warp.

Where one colour meets another, each weft turns back on itself, leaving a small slit. That is why kilim patterns are built from steps and diagonals, not curves.

In Anatolia, kilims were mostly woven by women at home. A girl wove them for her dowry, and they were folded into her dowry chest when she married.

How kilims are dyed

The wool was dyed with plants. Madder root gives the reds, indigo the blues, and walnut husks the browns. Vine leaves and chamomile give the yellows.

Each batch of dye comes out a little different. When one ran out partway through a rug, the colour shifts in a band across it. Weavers call this abrash.

The chief and the kilim

A Yörük chief was passing a tent one day when he noticed a kilim lying on the ground, and something in it troubled him. He sent for the weaver’s father and asked, “You want to marry your daughter to a man she doesn’t love, don’t you?”

The father was stunned, because it was true. He was poor, a rich man had asked for her, and she was in love with a poor young man. “How could you know?” he asked.

The chief pointed at the kilim. Then he gave the father a horse and a camel and told him to let his daughter marry the man she loved. “And tell her she wove it well,” he said. “A little less green next to the red, though. It almost misled me.”

Sources: Halıcızade, kilim.com, AA; the legend is retold from Mehmet Önder, “Anadolu’da Kilimler de Konuşur”, Kültür ve Sanat, 1999.
